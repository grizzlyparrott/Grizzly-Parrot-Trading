What Is NQ? Nasdaq-100 Futures Explained

By Kyle Parrott · Updated August 24, 2026

NQ is not the Nasdaq-100 itself. The Nasdaq-100 is an index calculated under Nasdaq’s methodology; NQ is CME’s dated, cash-settled E-mini futures contract that references that index. Keeping the index and the contract separate clarifies what a position represents, how it ends and which risks the chart does not show.

The reference portfolio

The Nasdaq-100 Is a Methodology, Not a List Nickname

Nasdaq defines the index as designed to measure 100 of the largest Nasdaq-listed non-financial companies and applies modified market-capitalization weighting. Eligibility, multiple security classes, reconstitution, rebalancing and corporate-action treatment follow the published methodology.

QuestionMethodology answerCommon shortcut to reject
What does it measure?Large Nasdaq-listed non-financial companies meeting published eligibility rules“The whole Nasdaq market”
How is it weighted?Modified market capitalization under methodology-defined constraints“Every company counts equally”
Is it exactly 100 securities?It targets 100 companies; multiple eligible securities from one company and temporary counts above 100 are possible“The index always has exactly 100 ticker lines”
Does it contain financial companies?No, under the current non-financial design“It represents every major sector in the same way as the S&P 500”
Is it permanently tech?No; use current official constituents, weights and classifications“NQ is a pure technology future”
Index composition changes.

Date every constituent, weight and concentration statement. The name Nasdaq-100 remains while the securities, weights and methodology can evolve.

The tradable agreement

NQ Wraps the Index Exposure in a Standardized Futures Contract

CME Rulebook Chapter 359 defines E-mini Nasdaq-100 futures. A buyer or seller takes a leveraged, marked-to-market contract position for a listed month; they do not buy or short each constituent security. Open positions that reach expiration are cash-settled under the exchange’s special-opening-quotation procedure.

Exposure

Dated Nasdaq-100 price risk

The futures price incorporates time to the contract date, financing, expected dividends and market supply and demand.

Clearing

Performance bond and variation

Margin supports obligations and can change. It is not a down payment, maximum loss or guarantee against liquidation.

Settlement

Cash, not 100-company delivery

The contract settles financially under CME rules; no basket of Nasdaq-100 shares is delivered to an expiring long.

The exact multiplier, tick value, listed months, session, termination, settlement calculation, roll and margin boundaries belong in the canonical NQ and MNQ mechanics guide.

A month and year belong to every position

Follow NQ From Listing to Cash Settlement

List

Several dated contracts exist

Confirm ticker root, month and year; a continuous chart is not an orderable contract.

Trade

Orders meet on the futures venue

Spread, depth, queue, event and intended size determine execution quality.

Mark

P&L changes account equity

Settlement variation and broker controls can require funds or liquidation before the thesis ends.

Roll or exit

Exposure moves or closes

Volume can migrate between months. A roll is two legs with basis and transaction costs.

Expire

Open positions settle in cash

CME uses the Nasdaq-100 special opening quotation under the applicable final-settlement procedure.

The nearest contract is not always the correct contract.

Use the actual liquidity migration, strategy horizon and broker cutoff. Never assume a platform’s continuous symbol is the month submitted to the exchange.

Possible purposes, not inferred motives

Who Might Use NQ Futures?

Hedger

Offset measured Nasdaq-100-like exposure

A hedge requires beta, amount, horizon, basis, roll, liquidity and residual-risk analysis. One NQ is not a perfect hedge for an arbitrary technology portfolio.

Asset allocator

Adjust index exposure with a futures overlay

The user must manage leverage, collateral, cash flows, roll and tracking differences from the target portfolio.

Relative-value researcher

Compare index exposures

NQ can be studied against ES or sector baskets only after normalizing returns, notional, weights, basis and costs.

Directional trader

Express a testable market view

The contract supplies exposure, not a signal. A trade still needs entry, invalidation, size, event and impairment rules.

Motive boundary: transaction and position data do not reveal a participant’s private purpose by themselves. Avoid labeling a price move as hedging, speculation or “smart money” without evidence.

Six risk families

The Index Name Does Not Show the Full Loss Path

Directional risk

The Nasdaq-100 can move against the position through company, macro or global information.

Concentration risk

Unequal current weights can make a subset of securities materially influence the index.

Leverage risk

Performance bond supports larger notional exposure; losses can exceed the deposit.

Execution risk

Spread, gaps, impact, partial fills, halts and rejects can worsen the modeled exit.

Lifecycle risk

Wrong month, failed roll or settlement proximity can create unintended exposure.

Basis and hedge risk

Futures can differ from the cash index or target portfolio because of carry, composition and timing.

The shortest safe learning path

Move From Index Identity to Simulated Operations

  1. Read the index methodology.Explain eligibility, modified weighting, company/security distinction and maintenance.
  2. Read the current CME rules.Verify the dated contract, multiplier, tick, hours, limits and final settlement.
  3. Translate risk.Calculate notional and whole-contract loss at a valid stop plus costs and stress.
  4. Inspect the book.Measure spread, usable depth, impact, session and roll state at intended size.
  5. Write the branches.Entry, partial fill, stop, event, halt, disconnect, roll, exit and no-trade.
  6. Rehearse in simulation.Demonstrate accurate position, P&L, order-state and expiry handling before live risk.

Readiness boundary

If you cannot distinguish NDX from NQ and a continuous chart from a dated contract, stay in simulation

Orientation is necessary but not sufficient. The contract becomes usable only when mechanics, loss limits and operational controls are reproducible.
