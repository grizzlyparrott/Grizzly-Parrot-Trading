02 · Methodology

Evidence first. Interpretation second.

Research is designed to show its work. A reader should be able to identify the source, understand the calculation, see the limits, and decide whether the conclusion is supported.

The question is not whether a market story sounds plausible. It is what the record establishes.

  1. Define the question and scope

    Set the market, instrument, dates, event definition, frequency, and comparison before drawing a conclusion.

  2. Start with the controlling source

    Use the government, central bank, exchange, regulator, or first-party record responsible for the fact whenever one is available.

  3. Make calculations inspectable

    Identify the input series, transformations, formulas, windows, and rounding so calculated results can be reproduced or challenged.

  4. Keep categories distinct

    Documented facts, observable data, calculated values, market interpretation, and practical implications are not treated as interchangeable.

  5. State what the evidence misses

    A daily observation does not become an intraday timestamp. A cash-market proxy does not become a continuous futures backtest. Missing evidence stays missing.

  6. Avoid promises of certainty

    Historical relationships and event responses are presented as evidence within a defined sample—not as guaranteed forecasts or trade signals.

Sources commonly used: government and central-bank records; exchange specifications and rulebooks; regulatory material; official historical datasets; public market data; contract documentation; and other first-party records relevant to the question.

Documented fact

A claim directly supported by an authoritative record, specification, release, or rule.

Observed or calculated

A value taken from data or derived with a disclosed method and defined sample.

Interpretation

An explanation of what the evidence may mean, kept separate from what it proves.

05 · The publication

About Grizzly Parrot Trading

An independent publication and searchable market library built around detailed research, long-form work, and practical market education.

Research is the publication’s central identity. Grizzly Parrot Trading studies how futures and related markets are structured, how contracts and institutions shape the available evidence, and how liquidity, policy, execution, and risk interact.

The broader library also includes focused educational guides, calculators, and reference material. Those resources translate market mechanics into usable terms without turning the publication into a promise of predictions, shortcuts, or certainty.

Authority here comes from the work itself: source-linked claims, defined samples, disclosed calculations, careful wording, and visible limitations.
